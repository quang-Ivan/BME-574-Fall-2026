- What did your VAE score, and what did the best two-line baseline score? State the spread across seeds and say whether the gap is bigger than it.
  - Our VAE had an AUC of 0.979, which is slightly lower than the Mahalanobis (0.985) and the best one, one-class SVM (0.986). The PCA reconstruction was slightly worse at 0.956. 
  - We trained with three different seeds (0, 1, and 2), and the negative ELBO converged to quite similar values at the training endpoint of 300 epochs, all just over 28, with a spread of around 0.17. This spread is much smaller than the standard deviation across rows, which is 41.7. 
- Which anomaly class was hardest to detect, and why might that be — what would the VAE have to fail to reconstruct in order to flag it?
  - We only have one anomaly class–the malignant class. The per-class AUC was equal to the pooled AUC (0.9788). Some of the malignant aspirates have larger nuclei, but others only have mildly irregular nuclei. To flag this, VAE would have to fail to reconstruct the modest changes in size of nuclei, radius and area, and concavity. Those values are in the tail of the benign variation, and thus the decoder reconstructs them well.
- What did your residuals imply? What was the naive loss assuming instead, and which direction did that push the balance between the two loss terms?
  - The residuals implied a smaller variance in the normal data. The naive loss assumed a variance of 0.5 which was 4.8 times the size that it actually was (0.10467), which means the KL-divergence term was too large.
- The supervised baseline used labels and the VAE did not. Given the gap, when would you still choose the unsupervised model in a real clinical setting?
  - In a clinical setting, because the unsupervised VAE performs worse than a model trained with labeled data, it would not be a good first-line system by comparison because the goal clinically is always to have the best outcome. However, it may be useful as an additional check to try to catch outliers not present in the original training data, which a purely labeled system may not do effectively. 
- You reported ELBO, which is an upper bound on the surprise, not the surprise itself. Does that matter for ranking examples? Does it matter for reporting an absolute number?
  - The score is -ELBO as an upper bound. The bound ranks cases correctly only if the gap is similar across cases. The encoder never saw malignant cases, so its posterior is likely worse on them, which increases their scores. This fact improves detection, but also means that we rank by surprise plus encoder error, not by likelihood. This does matter for reporting absolute numbers. The value is not the same as the likelihood and the value depends on sigma^2. Changing sigma^2 from 0.5 to 0.113 shifts every score and reweights both terms in the ELBO.
- Your training split contained no anomalies at all. Name one thing that could go wrong in deployment that this design would be unable to warn you about.
  - If the anomaly data falls within a subset of the distribution of the training data, such as a tighter sharper cluster in a single region of the training data. It would likely not be classified as anomalous by the VAE. This is a broader issue of unlabeled data rather than specific to data lacking anomalies.
